## Why Drive the Red Cloud Loop

There is a moment on the Red Cloud Loop — usually somewhere after the pavement gives way to gravel and the sagebrush gives way to aspen — when you realize you have not seen another car in a while, the air has turned cool and pine-scented, and the view has opened across miles of forest to the high Uintas. That moment is the whole reason to drive it. This is the route for travelers who want the mountains on the mountains' terms: quiet, wild, unhurried, and genuinely remote in a way the paved byway never quite is.

The Red Cloud Loop is a **Utah scenic backway**, and the word *backway* matters. It is not a manicured highway with a viewpoint every mile and a visitor center at the top; it is a long loop of paved and improved-gravel forest road that carries you up into the high country of <a href="/guides/ultimate-guide-to-ashley-national-forest/">Ashley National Forest</a> and back. Along the way it strings together the things the high Uintas do best — deep forest, mountain reservoirs, ridgeline overlooks, aspen groves, and abundant wildlife — with the added luxury of solitude. On a summer weekday you can have long stretches of it nearly to yourself.

What makes it special, beyond the scenery, is the **transition**. Few drives take you so completely from one world to another: you start in the hot, open, sage-and-juniper country near <a href="/guides/ultimate-guide-to-vernal-utah/">Vernal</a> and finish in cool alpine forest a mile or more higher, then reverse the whole journey on the way down. It is a drive that changes the temperature on your arm and the smell of the air, not just the view out the window. For photographers, fall-color chasers, wildlife watchers, and anyone who measures a good drive by how far it takes them from the everyday, the Red Cloud Loop delivers.

This guide is the definitive planning resource for driving it well — the route's shape, its landscape and ecology, where to stop, how to time it, what vehicle to bring, and how to stay safe on a remote mountain road. It deliberately does **not** re-tell the whole <a href="/guides/ultimate-guide-to-ashley-national-forest/">Ashley National Forest guide</a>; that cornerstone covers the forest as a destination — its recreation, campgrounds, and the broader Uintas — and this article links to it rather than repeating it. Here, the road itself is the subject. For the other great drives it connects to, and how the region's routes fit together, start with the <a href="/scenic-drives/">Scenic Drives in Dinosaur Country</a> pillar.

## Route Overview

Before you set out, understand the shape of the drive — because a remote loop rewards a driver who knows roughly what is coming and where the decisions are.

The **Red Cloud Loop is a loop**, as the name says: it leaves the Vernal area heading north into the mountains, makes a large circuit through the high country of Ashley National Forest, and returns toward town, so you are not backtracking. Its lower reaches pass through the **Dry Fork** country — a scenic canyon area near Vernal known for notable rock art — before the road climbs in earnest into forest. Up high, it connects to **mountain reservoirs**, forest side roads, trailheads, and ridgeline viewpoints, then descends by a different arm back toward the valley.

The surface is a **mix**: paved sections give way to long stretches of **improved gravel forest road**, and that combination is exactly what defines the loop's character. It is not a fast drive. Between the gravel, the grades, the curves, and the constant temptation to stop, it drives far more slowly than its mileage suggests — which is the point. Commonly cited at **roughly 70-plus miles**, it is a half-to-full-day undertaking once you factor in stops.

## Landscape & Ecology

The Red Cloud Loop is, more than anything, a drive through **change** — and understanding the landscape you are climbing through turns a pretty drive into a legible one. In the space of the loop you pass through several distinct life zones, stacked by elevation on the flank of the Uinta Mountains, and watching them shift past the window is one of the drive's quiet pleasures.

Down low, near Vernal and the Dry Fork country, you are in **high-desert sage and grassland** studded with **pinyon pine and juniper** — hot, open, and dry, with big skies and rocky canyon walls. As the road climbs, the junipers thin and the first **aspen** appear, their white trunks and shimmering leaves marking the transition into the mountains. Higher still, the aspen mix with and give way to **evergreen forest** — lodgepole pine, and, at the highest and coolest elevations, spruce and fir — cool, shaded, and fragrant. Interwoven through it all are **mountain meadows**, willow-lined creeks, and the reservoirs and lakes that dot the Uinta high country.

That layering is not just scenery; it is habitat, and it is why the loop is such good wildlife and bird country. Each zone supports different animals and plants, and the edges between zones — where meadow meets forest, or forest meets water — are where life concentrates. It is also why the loop photographs so richly in fall: the aspen zone lights up gold against the dark evergreens and the still-green meadows, a layered composition you simply do not get on a single-elevation drive.

Because you are climbing thousands of feet, you are also climbing into **cooler, wetter, more changeable weather**. The high country makes its own weather, and a warm, clear morning in Vernal can become a cool, cloudy, or stormy afternoon up top. That is a comfort in summer — the loop is a genuine escape from the desert heat — but it is also a planning reality. For the region's month-by-month patterns, see our <a href="/guides/vernal-weather-guide/">Vernal weather guide</a>, and pack layers regardless of the valley forecast.

## Forest & Alpine Scenery

If Sheep Creek is about rock and the byway is about the big reveal of Flaming Gorge, the Red Cloud Loop is about **forest** — the deep, cool, living green (and, in fall, gold) of the high Uintas. This is the drive to take when you want to be *among* the mountains rather than looking at them from a distance: under the aspen canopy, alongside a willow-lined creek, past a still reservoir reflecting the peaks, through meadows loud with birds.

The alpine character builds as you climb. The highest stretches of the loop carry you into cool evergreen forest and open meadows where the air is thin and clean and the light has a high-country clarity. Long views open where the forest breaks — across drainages, toward ridgelines, out over a landscape that feels genuinely wild. It is a restorative kind of scenery, the antidote to a hot desert day, and it is why the loop pairs so naturally with a picnic by the water or an unhurried afternoon of stopping wherever the view or the light demands.

This is high country, though, and its beauty comes with the responsibilities of remoteness. Weather changes fast, storms build in the afternoon, and the forest that feels so welcoming on a blue-sky morning is a serious place to be caught unprepared. Enjoy the alpine scenery fully — and respect what it asks of you: preparation, layers, and an eye on the sky. For the forest as a destination in its own right — its campgrounds, lakes, trails, and recreation — the <a href="/guides/ultimate-guide-to-ashley-national-forest/">Ashley National Forest guide</a> is your companion reference.

## Wildlife Viewing

The Red Cloud Loop is one of the best **wildlife drives** in the region, precisely because it moves slowly through so much varied, uncrowded habitat. The mix of high desert, aspen, evergreen forest, meadow, and water along the loop supports a genuine diversity of animals, and a quiet vehicle on a gravel road is an excellent way to see them.

Watch the forest edges and meadows for **mule deer**, which are common, and know that this is real **elk** country — bugling elk in fall are a Uinta signature. The high country is also **moose** habitat, and a moose in the willows along a creek or reservoir is a highlight many drivers remember above any view. Smaller mammals, a wealth of **birds** (including raptors overhead and songbirds in the aspen), and, down in the more open lower country, the chance of **pronghorn**, round out the cast. Around the reservoirs and creeks, watch for waterfowl and the animals that come to drink.

The rules for wildlife driving apply with extra force on a remote loop. Animals are most active at **dawn and dusk**, which is also when they are most likely to be on or near the road, so drive slowly at the edges of the day and be ready to stop. **Never approach, feed, or crowd wildlife** — use a zoom lens and watch from the vehicle. Treat every sighting as luck, not a promise; animals move seasonally and daily. And be genuinely alert to the **collision risk**: on a forested gravel road, a deer, elk, or moose can step out on a blind curve without warning, and help is far away — keep your speed down where cover is close to the road.

## Photography Tips

The Red Cloud Loop is a **photographer's drive**, and its variety is its gift: in a single loop you can shoot high-desert canyon, aspen forest, mountain reservoir, meadow, and ridgeline vista. A little planning around light and season turns a good drive into a great shoot.

- **Shoot the golden hours.** The low, warm light of early morning and late evening flatters everything here — forest, water, and especially fall aspen glowing against dark evergreens. It is also the best time for wildlife and the quietest time on the loop.
- **Chase the fall color.** In autumn, the aspen zone is the headline subject; look for layered compositions where gold aspen meet green evergreen and open meadow, and for reflections in the reservoirs. (See the Fall Colors section for timing.)
- **Use the reservoirs for reflections.** Calm mornings turn the mountain lakes into mirrors — peaks, forest, and sky doubled in still water.
- **Work the transition.** The desert-to-alpine change is itself a subject; a frame that captures sage-and-juniper below giving way to forest above tells the loop's whole story.
- **Keep a long lens ready.** For wildlife at the edges of the day and for isolating a distant ridgeline or a single blazing aspen stand, a zoom earns its place.
- **Watch the sky.** Building clouds and passing storms add drama to high-country shots — but the same weather is your cue to be cautious on remote roads.

<div class="article-tip"><strong>Two photo rules for this loop:</strong> first, always pull completely off at a safe, wide spot to shoot — never stop in the road on gravel or a blind curve. Second, protect the place and yourself: stay on the road and established pullouts, don't trample fragile meadows or approach wildlife, and keep an eye on the weather and the time — the light you're chasing at dusk is a long, dark, remote drive from town.</div>

## Family-Friendly Stops

The Red Cloud Loop can be a wonderful **family adventure** — but it is a different proposition from a short paved drive, and setting expectations makes it work. The appeal for kids is real: forest and meadows to explore, reservoirs to skip stones at, the genuine thrill of a remote gravel road, and good odds of spotting deer, elk, or even a moose. The sense of *going somewhere wild* is itself the attraction.

The winning approach is to treat it as a **relaxed, all-day outing** rather than a quick loop: keep driving segments broken up by frequent stops at the reservoirs, meadows, and overlooks; let kids get out, stretch, and explore safely; and build in a picnic by the water. The variety of the landscape — desert to forest to lake — helps hold young attention across a long day.

The cautions are the flip side of the loop's remoteness. It is **long, partly unpaved, and far from services, restrooms, and cell coverage**, so pack far more water and snacks than you think you need, bring layers for the cool high country, plan around naps and meals, and carry a basic kit for a remote drive. Keep speeds low on the gravel, and keep kids close near water and wildlife. Families wanting a shorter or easier first outing might prefer the paved overlooks of the <a href="/scenic-drives/flaming-gorge-uintas-scenic-byway/">Flaming Gorge–Uintas Byway</a> or a quick reservoir run; the Red Cloud Loop rewards families ready for a bigger day. For more kid-focused ideas around the region, see <a href="/things-to-do/">things to do in Vernal</a>.

## Fall Colors

If you can time it, **fall may be the single best reason to drive the Red Cloud Loop.** When autumn moves through the Uinta high country, the aspen turn brilliant gold and the whole forested heart of the loop lights up — mile after mile of color layered against dark evergreens, still-green meadows, and the blue of the reservoirs. Combined with crisp air, clear light, elk bugling in the distance, and thin crowds, a fall drive here is one of the great scenic experiences of the region, and it is why the loop is a natural anchor for a future **fall-color drives** guide.

The catch, as always in high country, is **timing and access**. Peak color is a moving target that depends on elevation and the year's weather, and it generally arrives higher up first and works downslope. Fall is also exactly when the loop begins heading toward its seasonal close, and early storms can arrive, so the very color you came for may coincide with narrowing access and changeable weather. The move is to go **earlier in the fall window** for the best combination of open road and turning leaves, to drive in the **golden hours** for the richest color, and to **VERIFY WITH OFFICIAL SOURCE** for both current color conditions and current road status before you commit a day to it. Because the loop is long and remote, give yourself margin — you do not want to be chasing the last light far from town on a cold, deteriorating gravel road.

## Accessibility

Scenic driving is one of the more accessible ways to experience high country, since much of the Red Cloud Loop's reward comes from the car and from stops close to the road. A traveler who cannot manage rough trails can still take in the forest, the reservoirs, the meadows, and the ridgeline views by driving the loop slowly and stopping at wide, level pullouts and developed sites.

That said, the Red Cloud Loop is a **remote, partly unpaved mountain road**, and it is not a uniformly accessible corridor. The gravel surface, the grades, small or uneven pullouts, and the distance from services all raise the bar compared with a paved, developed drive. Developed campgrounds and day-use sites vary in the accessible facilities they offer, and any feature reached by walking over rough ground — a lakeshore, a viewpoint, a trail — may not accommodate all mobility needs.

Because specific accessible facilities, site access, and current road conditions change site to site and season to season, travelers with mobility needs should **VERIFY WITH OFFICIAL SOURCE** (the U.S. Forest Service) for current accessible provisions at a specific campground, day-use area, or viewpoint before a visit. Travelers who want the high-country scenery with more developed, paved, and accessible facilities may find the <a href="/scenic-drives/flaming-gorge-uintas-scenic-byway/">Flaming Gorge–Uintas Byway</a> and its overlooks a better match.

## Vehicle Advice

Matching your vehicle to the Red Cloud Loop is the most important planning decision you'll make, because this is a **long, remote, partly unpaved drive** where the wrong vehicle turns a great day into a stressful — or stuck — one. Here is the honest breakdown.

**Passenger cars** can often manage the loop **when it is open, dry, and well-maintained**, driven slowly and carefully. But much of the loop is improved gravel, and a car is more vulnerable to a rough or wet surface, so this is conditional, not guaranteed — and after rain or early in the season the surface can deteriorate fast. If you drive a car, go when conditions are good, keep your speed down, and be willing to turn around.

**Higher-clearance and 4WD vehicles** are the **best tool** for the loop. They handle the gravel, the grades, and any rough or wet sections with the most confidence and comfort, and they open up the loop's side roads and forest spurs. If you have the choice, this is the vehicle to bring, especially outside the dry peak of summer.

**RVs, trailers, and large rigs** are generally the **wrong choice** for the Red Cloud Loop. It is long, narrow in places, unpaved, and far from services — a poor fit for a big vehicle. RV travelers are far better served by the paved <a href="/scenic-drives/flaming-gorge-uintas-scenic-byway/">byway</a> and the developed campgrounds at <a href="/guides/ultimate-guide-to-flaming-gorge/">Flaming Gorge</a>. If you must, confirm current conditions and any restrictions first — but the honest advice is to leave the big rig behind and drive the loop in something smaller.

## Motorcycle Tips

For the right rider on the right bike, the Red Cloud Loop is a memorable ride — but it is **not the same proposition as the paved byway**, and honesty matters. The paved sections offer the sweeping grades, cool forest air, and constant scenery that make Uinta riding so good. The **improved-gravel sections**, however, are a different discipline: loose surface, washboard, and remoteness that reward dual-sport and adventure bikes and experienced riders far more than street bikes.

If you ride the loop, ride it smart. **Watch the surface** — gravel, washboard, and debris washed onto the road demand respect and lower speeds. **Watch for wildlife** stepping into the road, especially in the forest at dawn and dusk. **Watch the weather** — high-country storms build fast and can turn a warm ride cold, wet, and slick in minutes, and wet gravel is treacherous. And **watch your fuel and supplies** — there are no services on the loop, so fuel up in town and carry what you need, including layers for the temperature drop up high. Riders wanting a pure paved experience will be happier on the <a href="/scenic-drives/flaming-gorge-uintas-scenic-byway/">Flaming Gorge–Uintas Byway</a>; the Red Cloud Loop is for riders who want the backcountry and have the bike and skills for gravel.

Check the forecast and road conditions, fuel up in town, dress for temperatures well below what Vernal shows, and <strong>VERIFY WITH OFFICIAL SOURCE</strong> for current road status before you ride.</div>

## Half-Day Itinerary

Short on time, or want a taste of the high country without committing to the full remote loop? A **half day** works well if you drive the loop's more accessible lower-and-mid reaches as an out-and-back rather than the whole circuit.

**The plan:** Leave <a href="/guides/ultimate-guide-to-vernal-utah/">Vernal</a> in the morning with a full tank, food, and water. Head up toward the loop through the **Dry Fork** country, taking in the scenic canyon and — if you've arranged access — its rock art. Climb into the forest as far as the pavement and your time comfortably allow, stopping at the **forest-transition pullouts** and, if you reach them, a **mountain reservoir** for a break, a picnic, and photos. Turn around when your half day is half gone and return the way you came, downhill toward Vernal, with the light behind you.

This gives you the desert-to-forest transition, some high-country scenery, and a reservoir stop without the full remote circuit, the gravel high sections, or the all-day commitment. It is the ideal choice for a first taste, a family testing the waters, or a day you're splitting with something else around town. Save the complete loop for a day when you have the hours — and the right vehicle — to do it properly. **VERIFY WITH OFFICIAL SOURCE** that your intended sections are open before you go.

## Full-Day Itinerary

With a **full day**, you can drive the complete Red Cloud Loop and do the high country justice — the transition, the forest, the reservoirs, the ridgelines, and the wildlife, at an unhurried backcountry pace.

**The plan:** Start early from <a href="/guides/ultimate-guide-to-vernal-utah/">Vernal</a>, fueled and provisioned, ideally in a **higher-clearance vehicle**. Climb through the **Dry Fork** country into the forest, watching the landscape shift from desert to aspen to evergreen. Take your time through the **high country** — stop at the reservoirs for a picnic and photos, pull off at the ridgeline overlooks, and drive the forested stretches slowly for wildlife, especially in the cooler morning and evening hours. Complete the **loop** back toward Vernal by a different arm, timing the descent so you're not caught far from town after dark. If the color is on in fall, plan the day around the golden hours in the aspen zone.

This is the complete experience: a full ecological transition, hours of high forest and mountain water, long views, solitude, and real wildlife country, all on one loop. Pace it generously — the loop rewards lingering, and the remoteness means you never want to be rushing on gravel as the light fails. Confirm the entire loop is open and in good condition before you build the day around it: **VERIFY WITH OFFICIAL SOURCE**. Want to make it a weekend? Pair the loop with the paved <a href="/scenic-drives/flaming-gorge-uintas-scenic-byway/">byway</a> and time at <a href="/guides/ultimate-guide-to-flaming-gorge/">Flaming Gorge</a>, or a day of <a href="/fishing/">fishing</a> a mountain reservoir.

## How the Red Cloud Loop Compares

The Red Cloud Loop is one of several great drives in Dinosaur Country, and knowing how it differs from its siblings helps you choose the right one — or the right order. Two comparisons come up most.

### Red Cloud Loop vs Sheep Creek Geological Loop

These two are the region's classic scenic side loops, but they could hardly be more different in scale and focus. The <a href="/scenic-drives/sheep-creek-geological-loop/">Sheep Creek Geological Loop</a> is **short, paved-ish, and intensely focused** — a spectacular hour or two of tilted-rock geology in a deep canyon near Flaming Gorge. The **Red Cloud Loop** is **long, remote, and expansive** — a half-to-full-day backcountry circuit of high forest, reservoirs, and ridgelines. Sheep Creek is a geology showcase you add to a byway day; Red Cloud is a destination drive in its own right.

## Planning Tips

A great Red Cloud Loop drive comes down to preparation, because remoteness is the whole character of the route. Keep these in mind:

- **Confirm it's open and in good shape first.** The loop is seasonal and unpaved in stretches — assume nothing. Check current status and surface before you build a day around it. <strong>VERIFY WITH OFFICIAL SOURCE.</strong>
- **Bring the right vehicle.** A higher-clearance vehicle is the right tool; a passenger car works only in good, dry conditions; a big RV generally does not belong here.
- **Fuel and provision fully in town.** Fill up in <a href="/guides/ultimate-guide-to-vernal-utah/">Vernal</a> and carry more water, food, and layers than you expect — there are <em>no</em> services on the loop.
- **Don't count on cell service.** Tell someone your route and expected return, and carry a paper map or offline maps.
- **Respect the weather and the clock.** Afternoon thunderstorms are common in summer; wet gravel is slick; and you never want to be finishing a long remote drive after dark. Start early and build in margin.
- **Drive slowly and stop often.** The reward is in the reservoirs, meadows, overlooks, and wildlife — not in the miles. Give the loop a half to full day.
- **Watch for wildlife and other traffic.** Slow down at dawn and dusk and on blind curves; watch for log trucks and other backcountry drivers.
- **Pack layers and sun protection.** You climb into real elevation and changeable mountain weather. See our <a href="/guides/what-to-bring/">what to bring guide</a> and <a href="/guides/vernal-weather-guide/">Vernal weather guide</a>.
