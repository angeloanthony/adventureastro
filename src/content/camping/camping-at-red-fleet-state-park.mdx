## Why Camp at Red Fleet

Most people who plan a trip to the Vernal area book their nights around the big names — <a href="/dinosaur-national-monument/visiting-dinosaur-national-monument/">Dinosaur National Monument</a>, <a href="/guides/ultimate-guide-to-flaming-gorge/">Flaming Gorge</a> — and treat Red Fleet, if they hear of it at all, as a quick afternoon stop. As a place to *camp*, that's a missed opportunity. Red Fleet packs an unusually satisfying trip into a small, forgiving package: a developed campground beside a warm red-rock reservoir, ten minutes north of a full-service town, with genuine dinosaur footprints a paddle away and swimming, fishing, and paddleboarding all right at camp.

What makes it special as a camping destination is not scale — it's *ease and payoff*. Where camping at Flaming Gorge gives you big, deep, dramatic water and camping up in <a href="/guides/ultimate-guide-to-ashley-national-forest/">Ashley National Forest</a> gives you cool pine and solitude, camping at Red Fleet gives you the lowest-friction, highest-reward family night out in the region. You can be set up and on the water within minutes of arriving, and the very next morning your kids can be tracing a dinosaur's footprints across a slab of sandstone. Few campgrounds anywhere let you fall asleep under dark desert skies and wake up steps from that.

The setting earns it, too. The reservoir is cradled by the tilted Navajo sandstone formations — the "red fleet" that gives the park its name — that glow at sunrise and sunset and mirror in the water on a calm morning. It photographs like a far more famous park but rarely holds a crowd, and its compact, low-key layout is exactly what makes it forgiving for beginning campers and families: easy to navigate, easy to leave and return to, and easy to pair with the region's bigger attractions.

And camping is what unlocks Red Fleet's best hours. As a day trip it's a pleasant few hours; as an overnight it becomes something better. Staying at the campground lets you do the exposed trackway route in the cool of early morning, catch the reservoir when it's glass-calm for paddling and reflections, fish the dawn window from your own shoreline, and have the whole place nearly to yourself before and after the day crowd. This guide is about *how* to camp it well. For the full picture of the park itself — its history, geology, the trackway story, and everything to do — pair it with the <a href="/guides/ultimate-guide-to-red-fleet-state-park/">Ultimate Guide to Red Fleet State Park</a>, and for how Red Fleet fits among the region's other camping, start at the <a href="/camping/">camping hub</a>.

## Camping Areas Overview

Red Fleet is refreshingly simple compared with the sprawling reservoir and forest destinations elsewhere in the region. Rather than a scattering of campgrounds across dozens of miles, it's a single, compact state park built around one reservoir — which means "where to camp at Red Fleet" is less about choosing among distant zones and more about choosing the right *site* within one campground, and about deciding whether the park itself is your best base or whether you'd rather stay in town and day-trip in.

Broadly, camping here breaks down into a few options:

- **The Red Fleet campground.** The park has a developed campground near the reservoir — the on-site option, and the one that delivers the signature experience of a red-rock lakeside night with the water minutes from your tent. This is the heart of camping at Red Fleet and the focus of this guide.
- **Lakeside versus set-back sites.** Within the campground, some sites sit closer to the water and the views while others are more set back and sheltered. Which is best depends on your priorities — dawn paddling and reflections versus a bit more shade and shelter. Exactly which sites sit where, and how close the water is, can shift with reservoir levels and management.
- **Nearby state-park camping at Steinaker.** Red Fleet's neighbor a few minutes back toward town, <a href="/guides/ultimate-guide-to-steinaker-state-park/">Steinaker State Park</a>, offers a second warm-reservoir campground — a natural alternative or overflow if Red Fleet is full, and an easy pairing on a longer trip (see the comparison below).
- **Vernal as a basecamp.** For families with very young kids, travelers hitting a rainy stretch, or anyone who wants a hot shower and a restaurant at the end of the day, basing in <a href="/guides/ultimate-guide-to-vernal-utah/">Vernal</a> — ten minutes south, with hotels, private RV parks, and full services — and day-tripping to Red Fleet is a comfortable alternative to sleeping at the park.

The practical takeaway for a first trip: **the park is small enough that the main decision is simply which site to reserve and how early** — so book ahead for summer, and let the size work in your favor. Because campsites, their layout, and their amenities change, and because reservoir levels move the shoreline, <strong>VERIFY WITH OFFICIAL SOURCE</strong> (Utah State Parks) for the current campground details and site specifics before you rely on any of them.

<div class="article-tip"><strong>Match camp to plan:</strong> a paddling-and-trackway trip is happiest with a site that gets you to the water fast for a calm-morning launch; a family swimming trip wants easy access to the beach and shade for the midday heat; a photography trip wants a clear line to the "fleet" formations for sunrise and sunset. ## Developed Campgrounds

For camping at Red Fleet, the developed park campground is the option, and it's a good one. A developed campground here means a designated site with basic facilities nearby — typically the kind of amenities a Utah State Park provides, set in the reservoir's red-rock scenery, within easy reach of the water, the beach, and the trackway trailhead. It's the backbone of camping at Red Fleet and, for the great majority of visitors, exactly the right choice.

The appeal is straightforward: you get the park's scenery and water access without the demands of primitive camping, in a managed, family-friendly setting that works for tents and RVs alike and sits minutes from town. Because Red Fleet is a developed state park, the campground is gentler and more predictable than the rustic forest campgrounds up in the Uintas or the more spread-out reservoir campgrounds at Flaming Gorge — which is a large part of why it's such a good place to camp with kids or to cut your teeth as a beginning camper.

What varies from site to site — and what this guide will never invent for you — is the specifics: **how many sites** the campground has, **which (if any) offer hookups**, whether there's a **dump station** and **potable water**, the **maximum site length**, whether sites are **reservable or first-come, first-served**, the **fees**, and the **seasonal opening and closing dates**. All of these are set by Utah State Parks, and they change year to year. Red Fleet is also a fee park — with day-use, camping, and boat-launch charges — and a Utah State Parks pass may cover day use; confirm what applies to your trip.

## RV Camping

Red Fleet is an easy, low-stress RV destination, and for many families it's the most comfortable way to enjoy the park — your own bed, kitchen, and climate control at the end of a hot high-desert day, minutes from town if you need anything. As a developed state park, the campground can generally accommodate RVs and trailers, and the short, paved approach off US-191 makes it far gentler to reach than the winding forest roads up in the Uintas. For RV travelers who want a warm-water reservoir, a dinosaur adventure, and none of the logistics of a big, remote destination, Red Fleet is hard to beat.

The variables that matter most to RVers are exactly the ones that change most often and that we'll never invent for you, so treat every one as something to confirm rather than assume:

- **Hookups** — whether a given site offers electric, water, or sewer connections. Developed state-park campgrounds are often more basic than private RV parks, so plan to be self-contained unless you've confirmed otherwise.
- **Dump station and potable water** — availability and location vary and can change; carry your own water if you haven't confirmed a source.
- **Maximum site length and pull-through sites** — a compact park's sites can be shorter and tighter than a big reservoir's loops, so bigger rigs should confirm they'll fit before committing.
- **Reservations** — RV-suitable sites are the first to fill on summer weekends; reserve early where sites are reservable.

## Tent Camping

For tent campers, Red Fleet is a gem: you can pitch within sight of the red-rock reservoir, wake to the "fleet" formations catching the first light, and be on the calm morning water — or on the trackway trail — before the day warms and the crowds arrive. The developed campground offers designated tent sites with facilities nearby, and the park's compact, forgiving layout makes it one of the best places in the region to tent-camp with kids or as a beginner.

The thing to plan around is the **high-desert environment** rather than the tent itself. This is exposed, high-elevation desert country: summer days can be hot and intensely sunny, nights can turn surprisingly cool even after a scorching afternoon, and wind and the occasional thunderstorm build in the afternoons. Stake well, bring a warmer sleeping bag than the midday heat suggests, and set up with an eye on both sun and wind. Shade is scarce in this open country, so a site with some natural shelter — or a good shade shelter of your own — earns its keep through the heat of the day.

Tent camping is the most flexible, lowest-cost way to experience Red Fleet, and it puts you closest to the water and the light for the dawn paddle and the golden hour. It's also the classic first-camping-trip setup here, because the park is small, close to town, and easy: a forgiving place to shake down new gear or introduce kids to a night outdoors. If you want cooler nights and forest shade instead of open red rock, the <a href="/camping/camping-in-ashley-national-forest/">Ashley National Forest</a> campgrounds are a short drive up the elevation; if you want bigger water, <a href="/camping/camping-at-flaming-gorge/">Flaming Gorge</a> is an hour north. ## Family Camping

Red Fleet may be the single most family-friendly place to camp near Vernal, and it earns that title by making camping *easy to succeed at* — the whole point when kids are involved. From one small campground your family can reach a warm reservoir to swim in, calm water to paddle, gentle shoreline to fish, and a slab of real dinosaur footprints to walk across, with town just ten minutes away for the inevitable forgotten item. It hits nearly every kid priority at once, in a low-stress, compact setting where nothing is a long drive or a big commitment.

The formula for a great family camping trip here is simple:

- **Keep the first trip short and close.** Red Fleet's ten-minute distance from Vernal is a feature — a spontaneous overnight or a first camping trip that can be aborted or resupplied without drama. For the very easiest introduction, its even-closer neighbor <a href="/guides/ultimate-guide-to-steinaker-state-park/">Steinaker</a> is a hair simpler; Red Fleet adds the dinosaur adventure that makes it stick.
- **Do the dinosaur tracks early.** The trackway route is exposed high desert with little shade, so tackle it in the cool morning with hats, sunscreen, and plenty of water — a small adventure that turns a camping trip into a story the kids remember.
- **Life jackets, always.** There are **no lifeguards**, and the water is colder below its warm surface. Keep non-swimmers and kids in life jackets on and near the reservoir, and supervise closely.
- **Let the day flex.** Alternate swimming, paddling, a shoreline fishing session, and quiet camp time; the park is small enough that you can shift plans on a whim as the heat and the kids' energy dictate.
- **Pack for sun and dryness.** Shade is scarce and the air is dry; bring more water, snacks, and sun protection than you think you'll use, and plan for warm days and cool nights.

Camping — rather than day-tripping — is what makes a family visit special here, because it gives you the cool, calm mornings and the quiet evenings that the day crowd never sees. ## Dinosaur Trackway Access

The dinosaur trackway is Red Fleet's headline attraction, and camping at the park is the best possible way to experience it. The full story of the tracks — the hundreds of roughly 200-million-year-old Early Jurassic footprints preserved on a sloping sandstone surface at the reservoir's edge, and how to see them — belongs to the <a href="/guides/ultimate-guide-to-red-fleet-state-park/">Ultimate Guide to Red Fleet State Park</a>. What matters *for campers* is timing and access: being camped on-site lets you reach the trackway at the one time it's genuinely comfortable and rewarding — early, cool morning, before the heat and the wind.

There are two common ways to reach the tracks, and camping improves both:

- **By trail.** A hiking route leads from a park trailhead to the trackway along the reservoir. It's the straightforward option, but it's an exposed, high-desert walk with little shade — which is exactly why campers have the advantage: you can be on the trail at first light, before the sun turns it into an oven, rather than arriving mid-morning after an hour's drive from town.
- **By water.** Many visitors paddle a kayak or paddleboard directly across the reservoir to the trackway shoreline, which shortens the overland walk and turns the visit into a small adventure. It's the local favorite when the water is calm — and calm water is a morning phenomenon here, another reason camping (and launching at dawn) beats day-tripping.

<div class="article-tip"><strong>Protect the tracks:</strong> the trackway is an irreplaceable scientific and cultural resource. Never touch, chalk, cast, wet, or pour anything on the prints to "enhance" them for a photo, don't stack rocks or add graffiti, and never attempt to remove anything — vandalism and theft have damaged sites like this. Look, photograph respectfully with natural low-angle light, and leave it exactly as you found it. Because the tracks are exposed at the water's edge, access can be affected by reservoir levels and conditions. <strong>VERIFY WITH OFFICIAL SOURCE</strong> (Utah State Parks) for the current trail distance, route, and access before you build a trip around it.</div>

The camper's move is simple: plan your trackway visit for your first morning, go at dawn while it's cool and the water is glassy, carry more water than you think you need, and treat the site with care. Then spend the warming rest of the day swimming, paddling, and fishing back at camp.

## Fishing From Camp

For a lot of campers, a shoreline fishing session at dawn and dusk is the quiet heart of a camping trip — and Red Fleet is built for exactly that. Camping puts you on the water at the low-light hours when fish are most active and the day crowd is gone, and the park's small, sheltered reservoir is an approachable, family-friendly place to fish from shore or a boat without the distances and logistics of a big water. It's not the trophy destination that Flaming Gorge is; it's something arguably more useful on a family camping trip — an easy, forgiving fishery where a kid can actually catch something from the bank.

Red Fleet is generally known for **rainbow trout** along with warm-water species such as **bass** and **bluegill**, which gives anglers variety through the seasons. Camping shapes how you fish it: from a lakeside site you can walk to the water for a morning cast before breakfast, and its compact size means shoreline anglers — not just boaters — have a real shot, which is perfect for kids and beginners. Fish the calm dawn and dusk windows from camp, and get off the open water if afternoon wind or a storm builds.

## Paddleboarding & Kayaking

Paddling is arguably the best thing you can do from a Red Fleet campsite — and, uniquely, it can double as your route to the dinosaur trackway. The reservoir's sheltered, compact size keeps the water calm on a still morning, the red sandstone formations make a spectacular backdrop, and gliding across the water toward a shoreline of 200-million-year-old footprints is the kind of experience that sticks with people long after the trip. For a camper, the payoff is timing: you're already on-site to launch at dawn, when the water is glassy and the light is best, instead of arriving mid-morning to wind and chop.

A few practicalities make camp-based paddling go smoothly:

- **Go early from camp.** Mornings are typically calmest; afternoon wind picks up and ripples the water, making both paddling and reflections harder. Being camped on the water is the whole advantage — use it for a first-light launch.
- **Life jackets on.** Cold water below the warm surface, no lifeguards, and open water mean life jackets for everyone, especially kids.
- **Bring your own, or check rentals.** Whether on-site rentals are available changes — <strong>VERIFY WITH OFFICIAL SOURCE</strong> (Utah State Parks) before counting on renting a kayak or paddleboard at the park; many campers bring their own.
- **Mind the trackway shoreline.** If you paddle to the tracks, land carefully and treat the site with the same respect and care as any hiker would.

Paddling is also the gentlest way to give kids a real sense of adventure from the safety of a calm, small reservoir — a natural centerpiece for a family camping trip. For the fuller paddling picture, the <a href="/guides/ultimate-guide-to-red-fleet-state-park/">Red Fleet destination guide</a> covers the reservoir recreation in more depth.

## Swimming

On a hot Uintah Basin summer day, Red Fleet's reservoir is a genuine relief, and swimming is one of the great pleasures of camping here. The water warms nicely through the season, there's a beach area for wading and sunning, and a red-rock setting beats any municipal pool — the kind of easy, cooling payoff that makes a hot camping trip worth it for the whole family. Camping means you can swim in the warm afternoon after an early trackway hike and a morning paddle, then cool off again at dusk when the crowd has gone.

The essential caution is the same one that governs everything at this reservoir: **there are no lifeguards.** Supervise children closely, keep non-swimmers in life jackets, and remember that water temperature drops with depth even when the surface feels warm. Swimming is a warm-season activity here — the water is often too cold for comfort outside the peak summer months. <strong>VERIFY WITH OFFICIAL SOURCE</strong> (Utah State Parks) for current designated swim areas, water conditions, and any seasonal advisories before swimming.

## Photography Tips

A camping trip at Red Fleet is also a photography trip, and camping is the secret weapon — it puts you on location for the golden hours and the dark skies, when the day-trippers are gone and the "fleet" glows.

- **Shoot the golden hours from camp.** Camping means you're already lakeside for sunrise and sunset, when the Navajo sandstone formations glow their most intense red; midday flattens the color. Early and late light is everything here, and only campers reliably catch both.
- **Chase reflections at dawn.** Calm morning water mirrors the red formations for the park's signature shot — get out before the wind picks up, which on this small reservoir is a genuinely early affair.
- **Use the paddle perspective.** A kayak or paddleboard puts you at water level for unique low-angle compositions of the formations and shoreline; protect your gear from the water.
- **Photograph the trackway respectfully.** Capture the footprints with natural light and a low angle to bring out their depth — but never chalk, wet, or touch the prints to "improve" a photo, which damages the resource.
- **Work the dark skies.** Away from town, Red Fleet delivers excellent star and Milky Way photography on clear, moonless nights — a real reason to camp rather than day-trip.
- **Watch the skies for safety.** Summer thunderheads build dramatic backdrops but bring lightning risk on open water and exposed rock — get off the water and off high ground when storms approach.

## Wildlife

Camping at Red Fleet puts you in high-desert-and-water country, and a few habits keep both you and the animals safe. The most important is **food storage**: keep all food, trash, and scented items secured — in a hard-sided vehicle or per the campground's posted rules — and never in your tent, so you don't draw animals into camp. Clean up thoroughly after every meal and pack out all scraps, and store gear so smaller animals don't chew their way in overnight.

For a small park, Red Fleet supports a nice range of wildlife. Around the sagebrush and rock you may see **mule deer**, **jackrabbits**, **cottontails**, **lizards**, and a variety of desert birds, while the reservoir draws **waterfowl** and other birds to its shoreline — a good spot for casual birdwatching in the soft light of morning and evening, exactly when campers are out. As always in the high desert, give animals their space and never feed wildlife. Watch where you put your hands and feet on rocky ground, since **rattlesnakes** live in the region, and watch for animals on the road at dawn and dusk when driving to and from camp.

<div class="article-tip"><strong>Note:</strong> specific wildlife-safety and food-storage requirements can vary by campground and area and may change with conditions. Follow all posted rules at your campground, give all wildlife plenty of room, and <strong>VERIFY WITH OFFICIAL SOURCE</strong> (Utah State Parks) for current requirements where you're camping.</div>

## Accessibility

As a developed state park, Red Fleet generally provides accessible parking and basic facilities in its day-use and campground areas, and much of the reservoir's shoreline and beach experience is close to parking — which tends to make the developed campground a more accessible camping choice than the primitive and dispersed sites in the forest or the more rugged corners of the big reservoir. The compact, largely paved-access layout is part of what makes the park gentle for a wide range of visitors.

That said, reaching the **dinosaur trackway is an unpaved, high-desert route over uneven ground**, and getting to the tracks — whether on foot or by paddle — involves terrain that is not uniformly accessible. Because specific accessible campsites and facilities vary and change over time, <strong>VERIFY WITH OFFICIAL SOURCE</strong> (Utah State Parks) for current accessibility provisions before a visit if you have specific needs.

## Weekend Camping Itinerary

A weekend is the classic Red Fleet camping trip, and two nights is plenty to do the park justice and still branch out to the wider region. This itinerary is built around the camper's great advantage: early, cool, calm mornings.

**Day 1 — Arrive, set up, and get on the water.** Leave <a href="/guides/ultimate-guide-to-vernal-utah/">Vernal</a> in the late morning — it's only ten minutes — set up at your reserved campground, and spend the warm afternoon on the reservoir: a swim off the beach, a paddle among the red-rock formations, or an easy shoreline <a href="/fishing/fishing-red-fleet-reservoir/">fishing</a> session with the kids. Cook dinner as the light goes soft and warm on the "fleet," and stay up for a sky full of stars.

**Day 2 — Dinosaurs at dawn, water by day.** Rise early and reach the **dinosaur trackway** while it's cool and the water is glass-calm — either hike the trail from camp or paddle across the reservoir to the site, photographing the prints respectfully. Back at camp, swim, paddle, and fish through the warm middle of the day, then enjoy a second red-rock sunset and a quiet night. With a car and a little energy, the afternoon is also a natural window to pair the tracks with the **fossils** at <a href="/dinosaur-national-monument/visiting-dinosaur-national-monument/">Dinosaur National Monument</a>, a short drive southeast, for the full dinosaur story.

**Day 3 — A last morning, then home or onward.** Take one more dawn on the water — a cast, a paddle, or coffee by the reservoir — break camp, and either make the easy drive back to Vernal or push on to a bigger day: north to <a href="/camping/camping-at-flaming-gorge/">Flaming Gorge</a> for big water, up into <a href="/camping/camping-in-ashley-national-forest/">Ashley National Forest</a> for cool mountain air, or a scenic loop on one of the region's <a href="/scenic-drives/">scenic drives</a>.

It's the quintessential easy-family weekend: two nights beside a warm red-rock reservoir, a dinosaur adventure at dawn, and the best light and quietest hours bookending each day. Driving in from the west? See our <a href="/from/salt-lake-city/">Salt Lake City to Vernal guide</a> for the approach.

## Planning Tips

Pull it all together with a simple plan:

- **Reserve early for summer.** The best and most water-adjacent sites fill on summer weekends and holidays. <strong>VERIFY WITH OFFICIAL SOURCE</strong> (Utah State Parks and the reservation system) for reservation windows and book ahead, keeping Steinaker or a private Vernal RV park as a backup.
- **Verify fees, amenities, and openings.** Red Fleet is a fee park with day-use, camping, and boat-launch charges, and its sites, hookups, water, and seasonal hours change year to year — never assume; confirm before you go.
- **Do the trackway in the cool morning.** The route is exposed high desert; go at dawn with sun protection and plenty of water, and treat the irreplaceable prints with care — look, photograph, and leave them untouched.
- **Keep life jackets on near the water.** There are no lifeguards and the water is colder below its warm surface; supervise kids closely on and near the reservoir.
- **Pitch and launch for the calm.** Mornings are calmest for paddling, reflections, and fishing; get on the water early and off it when afternoon wind or storms build.
- **Pack for high-desert swings.** Hot days, cool nights, intense sun, scarce shade, and afternoon wind are all in play — bring layers, sun protection, a shade shelter, and more water than you expect.
- **Always check current fire restrictions.** Bans are common in dry periods; never assume a campfire is allowed, and confirm the rule for the park before you build any fire.
- **Base in Vernal for supplies.** Fuel, groceries, tackle, and outdoor gear are ten minutes away in town — a great fallback when weather turns. Pack with our <a href="/guides/what-to-bring/">what to bring guide</a>.
- **Pair it up.** Red Fleet is at its best combined with the nearby state parks, the monument, or a mountain or big-water day — build it into a bigger <a href="/camping/">Dinosaur Country camping plan</a> rather than a standalone stop.

Camped with a plan, Red Fleet delivers a kind of trip few places can match for the effort involved — a warm red-rock reservoir minutes from town, a dinosaur adventure a paddle away at dawn, easy fishing and swimming and stargazing at camp, and rarely a crowd to share it with. Pick your site and reserve early, do the trackway in the cool morning, keep the kids in life jackets, verify the details that change, and let this small, generous park be the easy anchor of a wider Dinosaur Country trip. And when you want to trade the campsite for a guided backcountry adventure between nights on the water, <a href="/booking/">book a UTV tour with Adventure Tours Vernal</a> and let Dave and Trudy show you the country beyond the reservoir.
