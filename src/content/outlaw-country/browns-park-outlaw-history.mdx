<h1 class="article-title">Browns Park: The Valley Where Outlaws Disappeared</h1>

<div class="article-body">

<div class="article-summary-box"><strong>Quick Answer:</strong> <strong>Browns Park</strong> is a broad, remote valley on the <strong>Green River</strong> in the far northeastern corner of Utah, at the point where Utah, Colorado, and Wyoming meet. In the late 1800s it was one of the most effective hiding places in the American West — not because it was hard to find, but because of what its <em>geography</em> did for a wanted man: good winter grass, a river, few approaches, isolated neighbors who did not ask questions, and three separate state jurisdictions within a hard day's ride. Butch Cassidy and the Wild Bunch are documented in this valley. So are the Bassett family, whose ranch fed them, and John Jarvie, whose store and ferry were the valley's commercial center until he was murdered in 1909. You can visit: the <strong>John Jarvie Historic Ranch</strong> on the Utah side is a Bureau of Land Management historic property with original buildings still standing, and the Colorado side holds <strong>Browns Park National Wildlife Refuge</strong>. It is a long drive north of <a href="/guides/ultimate-guide-to-vernal-utah/">Vernal</a> with unpaved sections and no services — plan it as a full day, carry fuel and water, and <strong>VERIFY WITH OFFICIAL SOURCE</strong> for current routes, hours, and road conditions before you go.</div>

<h2>Where Browns Park Is, and Why That Was the Whole Point</h2>

<p><span style="display:inline-block;background:rgba(184,197,168,0.4);border:1px solid #B8C5A8;border-radius:999px;padding:2px 11px;font-size:0.8rem;font-weight:700;letter-spacing:0.04em;">DOCUMENTED</span></p>

<p>Browns Park is a long, open valley on the <strong>Green River</strong>, lying across the corner where <strong>Utah, Colorado, and Wyoming</strong> come together. It is north of Vernal, past the eastern end of the Uinta Mountains, in country that has never had a town in it. The older name was <strong>Brown's Hole</strong> — "hole" being the fur-trade word for a sheltered basin ringed by higher ground, the same usage that gave Wyoming its Jackson Hole.</p>

<p>Four features made it extraordinarily useful to men who did not want to be found, and none of them are romantic:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Three jurisdictions, one short ride apart.</strong> This is the decisive one. Law enforcement in the 1890s was county-based and slow, and a sheriff's authority stopped at a line on a map that a rider could cross in an afternoon. Coordinating a pursuit across two state boundaries meant telegrams, warrants, and days. In Browns Park, a man could put a state line between himself and a posse before the posse had finished saddling.</li>
<li><strong>Winter range.</strong> The valley's elevation and shelter give it milder winters than the high country around it, and good grass. That made it genuinely valuable ranching country — which meant there were legitimate reasons for cattle and riders to be there, and a working economy to disappear into.</li>
<li><strong>Very few ways in.</strong> Ringed by rough country and cut by the river's canyons, the valley had a small number of practical approaches. That cuts both ways: hard to reach, and easy to watch. You would know a stranger was coming long before he arrived.</li>
<li><strong>Neighbors with reasons of their own.</strong> The valley's settled families were remote, self-reliant, and frequently at odds with the large cattle interests pressing in from outside. Hospitality to a passing rider was normal; discretion about who he was could be a matter of local loyalty.</li>
</ul>

<p>Put those together and you do not have a hideout in the movie sense — no secret cave, no hidden pass. You have something far more effective: <strong>a place where a wanted man could live openly, work, be fed, and be twenty miles from a different state's problem.</strong></p>

<h2>The Outlaw Trail: What It Actually Was</h2>

<p><span style="display:inline-block;background:rgba(218,165,32,0.28);border:1px solid #DAA520;border-radius:999px;padding:2px 11px;font-size:0.8rem;font-weight:700;letter-spacing:0.04em;">ASSOCIATED</span></p>

<p>Here is something most sites will not tell you: <strong>nobody in the 1890s called it the Outlaw Trail.</strong> There was no marked route, no single track on the ground, and no rider set out to "ride the Outlaw Trail." <h2>The People</h2>

<h3>Butch Cassidy</h3>

<p><span style="display:inline-block;background:rgba(184,197,168,0.4);border:1px solid #B8C5A8;border-radius:999px;padding:2px 11px;font-size:0.8rem;font-weight:700;letter-spacing:0.04em;">DOCUMENTED</span></p>

<p>He was born <strong>Robert LeRoy Parker</strong> in 1866 in Beaver, Utah, the eldest child of Mormon settlers, and grew up on a hardscrabble ranch near Circleville. The surname he took came from <strong>Mike Cassidy</strong>, a rustler he worked alongside as a young man; the "Butch" came later. In June 1889 he took part in the robbery of the bank at <strong>Telluride, Colorado</strong>. He served time in the Wyoming Territorial Prison at Laramie in the mid-1890s for horse theft and was released in 1896. In April 1897 came the <strong>Castle Gate payroll robbery</strong> in Utah — a daylight seizure of a mine payroll that made his name. Then the train robberies: <strong>Wilcox</strong> in 1899, <strong>Tipton</strong> in 1900, and the bank at <strong>Winnemucca, Nevada</strong>, the same year. In 1901 he left for South America with Harry Longabaugh and Etta Place. He is generally accepted to have died in a shootout at <strong>San Vicente, Bolivia, in 1908</strong>, though that has never been conclusively established, and survival stories have circulated ever since.</p>

<p>His connection to Browns Park is not in dispute. The valley is repeatedly placed in his movements in the 1890s, and it functioned exactly as described above: a place to winter, to rest horses, and to be somewhere a Utah warrant did not reach.</p>

<h3>The Bassett Family</h3>

<p><span style="display:inline-block;background:rgba(218,165,32,0.28);border:1px solid #DAA520;border-radius:999px;padding:2px 11px;font-size:0.8rem;font-weight:700;letter-spacing:0.04em;">ASSOCIATED</span></p>

<p>Herb and Elizabeth Bassett settled in Browns Park in the late 1870s and raised their family there. Their ranch became known as a place where riders were fed — including, by consistent account, members of the <strong>Wild Bunch</strong>. Herb Bassett kept a library and an organ in a valley with neither town nor church; the household was, by the standards of the place, cultured and hospitable. We label this <em>associated</em> rather than <em>documented</em> because the hospitality is well attested in memoir and local history while individual visits are rarely pinned to a date.</p>

<p><strong>Ann Bassett</strong> (1878–1956) grew up to become the most famous woman in the valley's history — the <strong>"Queen of the Cattle Rustlers,"</strong> a title she did not much resist. She was tried for cattle rustling and acquitted, and she spent much of her life in open conflict with the large cattle operations pressing into Browns Park.</p>

<p>The persistent claim that Ann Bassett was secretly <strong>Etta Place</strong>, Sundance's companion, is <span style="display:inline-block;background:rgba(193,68,14,0.22);border:1px solid #C1440E;border-radius:999px;padding:2px 11px;font-size:0.8rem;font-weight:700;letter-spacing:0.04em;">LEGEND</span> — a wonderful theory, argued seriously by some, but not established fact.</p>

<h3>Josie Bassett Morris — the one you can reach easily</h3>

<p><span style="display:inline-block;background:rgba(184,197,168,0.4);border:1px solid #B8C5A8;border-radius:999px;padding:2px 11px;font-size:0.8rem;font-weight:700;letter-spacing:0.04em;">DOCUMENTED</span></p>

<p>Ann's older sister <strong>Josie Bassett Morris</strong> (1874–1964) is the reason this history sits closer to most visitors than they realize. She grew up on the Browns Park ranch in the years when the valley was at its wildest. Decades later she homesteaded a box canyon at <strong>Cub Creek</strong>, in what is now <a href="/dinosaur-national-monument/visiting-dinosaur-national-monument/">Dinosaur National Monument</a>, and lived there largely alone for roughly <strong>fifty years</strong> — raising stock, running a garden, and outlasting nearly everyone from the era she was born into.</p>

<p><strong>Her cabin still stands at the end of the <a href="/scenic-drives/cub-creek-road-tour-of-the-tilted-rocks/">Cub Creek road</a>.</strong> Thousands of people drive out to it every season, walk into the shade of Box Canyon, read the sign, and leave without ever learning that the quiet homesteader in the story grew up in the outlaw valley — or that her sister was tried for rustling. If you do nothing else on this page, do this: drive Cub Creek, stand at Josie's, and understand what you are looking at. It is twenty minutes from town, on a road you were probably going to drive anyway.</p>

<h3>John Jarvie</h3>

<p><span style="display:inline-block;background:rgba(184,197,168,0.4);border:1px solid #B8C5A8;border-radius:999px;padding:2px 11px;font-size:0.8rem;font-weight:700;letter-spacing:0.04em;">DOCUMENTED</span></p>

<p><strong>John Jarvie</strong> was a Scottish immigrant who settled in Browns Park around <strong>1880</strong> and built the valley's commercial center: a general store, a post office, and a <strong>ferry across the Green River</strong>. In a valley with no town, that made him the closest thing to one. He knew everyone who passed through, including men whose names are now famous, and by all accounts dealt with them as customers.</p>

<p>In <strong>1909</strong> he was <strong>murdered</strong> during a robbery at his own store, and his body was set adrift on the river, to be recovered downstream some time later. The crime was never satisfactorily resolved.</p>

<p>The <strong>John Jarvie Historic Ranch</strong> — managed today by the <strong>Bureau of Land Management</strong> — preserves the site, with original structures still standing. This is the anchor of any visit to Browns Park. It is not a reconstruction and not a themed attraction; it is the actual place, on public land, in the actual valley.</p>

<h3>Tom Horn and the killings of 1900</h3>

<p><span style="display:inline-block;background:rgba(218,165,32,0.28);border:1px solid #DAA520;border-radius:999px;padding:2px 11px;font-size:0.8rem;font-weight:700;letter-spacing:0.04em;">ASSOCIATED</span></p>

<p>The darker chapter, and the one that ended Browns Park's outlaw era, is not about outlaws at all — it is about what was sent to deal with them.</p>

<p>In <strong>1900</strong>, two Browns Park cattlemen were shot dead: <strong>Matt Rash</strong> and, weeks later, <strong>Isom Dart</strong>, a Black cowboy who had been born into slavery and was widely respected in the valley as a horseman. Both were killed after receiving warnings to leave. The killings are attributed by historians to <strong>Tom Horn</strong>, a former scout working as a stock detective for large cattle interests — but <strong>he was never charged with either death</strong>, which is why this section is labeled <em>associated</em> and not <em>documented</em>. Horn was hanged in Cheyenne in <strong>1903</strong> for a different killing, that of a fourteen-year-old boy named Willie Nickell, a conviction still argued over today.</p>

<p>The effect on Browns Park was immediate and permanent. A valley whose entire economy of refuge depended on being a place where nobody troubled you had become a place where you could be shot from cover for staying. Within a few years, the era was over.</p>

<div class="article-tip"><strong>An editorial note.</strong> We include Rash and Dart deliberately. The comfortable version of Western history stops at charming bank robbers; the real story of this valley includes a hired killing campaign and a Black cowboy, born enslaved, murdered for holding his ground. Leaving that out would make for lighter reading and worse history.</div>

<h2>Before the Outlaws: Fort Davy Crockett</h2>

<p><span style="display:inline-block;background:rgba(184,197,168,0.4);border:1px solid #B8C5A8;border-radius:999px;padding:2px 11px;font-size:0.8rem;font-weight:700;letter-spacing:0.04em;">DOCUMENTED</span></p>

<p>The outlaw decades were not the valley's first chapter. In the late 1830s, Brown's Hole held <strong>Fort Davy Crockett</strong>, a fur-trade post serving trappers and Indigenous traders in the closing years of the Rocky Mountain fur era. It was by contemporary account a rough and poorly supplied place, and it did not last long. Long before that — and continuing after — this was homeland to <strong>Ute and Shoshone</strong> peoples, whose presence in this country vastly predates every name on this page.</p>

<h3>Preparing for the drive</h3>

<ul>
<li><strong>Fuel up in Vernal.</strong> There is no fuel in Browns Park. Leave town with a full tank and plan the round trip on it.</li>
<li><strong>Carry water and food for everyone, plus extra.</strong> No services means no services.</li>
<li><strong>Assume no cell coverage.</strong> Download offline maps and bring a paper map. Tell someone your route and expected return time.</li>
<li><strong>Respect the unpaved sections.</strong> Dirt in this country is fine when dry and genuinely dangerous when wet. Rain or snowmelt can turn a manageable road into slick, impassable clay within an hour. If the forecast is wet, move the trip.</li>
<li><strong>Check your spare</strong> — tire, jack, and the tools to use them — before you leave pavement.</li>
<li><strong>Give it the whole day.</strong> Rushing this drive is how people get into trouble on it.</li>
</ul>

<h3>Seasonal timing</h3>

<p><strong>Late spring through fall</strong> is the practical window, with early fall the local favorite — cooler air, better light, and thinner traffic on the dirt. <strong>Summer</strong> is workable but hot and exposed, so start early. <strong>Winter</strong> is for people who know this country well; snow, mud, and short daylight can close the trip down entirely, and seasonal site closures are common. Because opening seasons and road status vary year to year, VERIFY WITH OFFICIAL SOURCE before any shoulder-season attempt.</p>

<h3>Families, and who this trip suits</h3>

<p>Browns Park itself is a <strong>long day in a vehicle</strong> — genuinely rewarding for children old enough to be gripped by the story, genuinely tedious for those who aren't. Be honest with yourself about which you have. Younger families get far more out of the <strong>Josie Morris cabin</strong> at Cub Creek: same family, same history, twenty minutes from town, shady canyon walks, and a national monument's worth of dinosaurs attached. Pair it with the <a href="/hiking/best-hikes-in-dinosaur-national-monument/">short walks near the cabin</a> and you have a full, easy, memorable day.</p>

<h3>Accessibility</h3>

<p>Historic sites of this era were not built for access, and remote public land is rarely retrofitted. Surfaces at the Jarvie site are historic and uneven, and the approach is unpaved. The <strong>Cub Creek road</strong> is the far more accessible way into this history — mostly paved, drivable, with much of the interest viewable from the car or a short walk. Confirm specific accommodations directly with the managing agency (VERIFY WITH OFFICIAL SOURCE).</p>

<h3>Photography</h3>

<ul>
<li><strong>Early and late light</strong> is not optional here — midday in this valley is flat and harsh, and the whole subject is texture: weathered wood, stone, river, sage.</li>
<li><strong>Shoot the geography, not just the buildings.</strong> The photograph that explains this story is the wide one — the valley, the river, the emptiness on every side. That is the image that makes a reader understand why it worked.</li>
<li><strong>Detail work rewards you.</strong> Historic structures give you tool marks, hinges, worn thresholds. Never touch, move, or climb on anything.</li>
<li><strong>Wildlife on the Colorado side.</strong> The refuge is a serious birding destination — bring the long lens.</li>
<li><strong>Leave it as you found it.</strong> These places are irreplaceable and largely unguarded. The reason they are still standing is that visitors have been careful.</li>
</ul>

<h2>Experience the Country Yourself</h2>

<p>The thing no exhibit conveys is the <em>scale</em> — how much empty, broken, beautiful country there is out here, and how completely it could swallow a rider who wanted to be swallowed. You do not have to reach Browns Park to feel that. The backcountry around Vernal is the same landscape: the same canyons, the same sage flats, the same distances.</p>

Three hours, $349 per machine, up to two riders.
